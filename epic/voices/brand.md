# The brand voice

How a product's own pages are written:  what it is, and why someone would want it, with nothing the reader can see
through.
Use it for a page someone reads to decide whether a thing is for them:
a home page, a feature page, a launch page.

The reader's own project adds its specifics on top:  its house style's writing rules, the names it never uses, its
checker.
Where the two disagree, the project's rules win.

## 1. Who's reading

Someone deciding, with a minute to spare.
- They may not be a developer:  they arrived from a link, a search, a friend.
- They've read a hundred product pages, and they know the moves:  the tagline, the "seamless", the waitlist.
  The first one they spot, they stop believing the page.
- They want four things, in this order:
  1. what it is, in one line they could repeat to someone else
  2. proof it's real:  a thing they can see working, or run in the next five minutes
  3. why they'd want it:  what changes for THEM
  4. the one next step, and what it costs (time, money, setup)

A second reader is the one who'll check:  a developer, a reviewer, a journalist.
They'll try what the page says.
Write for the first;  the second gets served by every claim being one they can run.

## 2. Eight habits

**1. A headline is a promise, and the next lines keep it.**
Every heading says something the reader wants.
The body under it proves it, on the same screen, before the reader can doubt it.
- Like this:  "Try it:  five commands, real output", then "Don't take this page's word for any of it.", then five
  commands, each with what it printed.
- Not this:  a heading that promises ("Three commands cover everything") over a body that only lists the three.
  A promise with no proof under it is the selling the reader came in braced for.

**2. Say what it is first, in the reader's words.**
One or two plain sentences, before any picture of the aspiration.
Name the thing by what the reader would call it, not by its category in the market.
- Like this:  "Spell is a programming language written in plain English."
- Not this:  "A next-generation platform for the future of software creation."

**3. Show it working before you say it's good.**
The first screen holds something real:  code, output, a screenshot, a short clip.
The praise, if any, comes after, and is usually unnecessary by then.
- Like this:  three lines of a real program, captioned with where it came from and what it compiles to.
- Not this:  three paragraphs of benefits, and the first example below the fold.

**4. Answer the suspicion in the next sentence.**
Every product page raises one obvious doubt.
Say it out loud, and answer it plainly, right where it comes up.
- Like this:  "That isn't pseudo-code, and it isn't a prompt.
  A rule-based parser reads it the same way every time:  no model guessing what you meant."
- Not this:  leaving the doubt for a FAQ at the bottom, which the doubter never reaches.

**5. Close the loop on the big idea.**
An aspiration is allowed:  people want to know where a thing is headed.
Mark what isn't built, and say which part of the dream they've already seen working.
- Like this:  a short list of aims, the unbuilt ones marked 🌱, then
  "The third one is real today:  you just watched FizzBuzz come out as Python."
- Not this:  a vision section with nothing marked, so the reader can't tell the product from the plan.

**6. Specific beats superlative.**
A number, a name, a time, a real command.
"Recompiles two seconds after you stop typing" says more than "instant", and the reader can check it.
- Use only numbers you measured, or that a page measured and you re-checked.
- An adjective with nothing behind it goes:  fast, powerful, seamless, effortless, next-generation.

**7. Short paragraphs, and confidence without volume.**
One to three sentences a paragraph;  a short one beside a longer one.
Confident means plain:  say it once, in the indicative, and stop.
- Like this:  "Six repos, and it still works like one project."
- Not this:  exclamation marks, "Really." after a claim, "simply", "just", "imagine".
  A word that asks to be believed is a word the reader stops believing.

**8. Make the next step small and honest.**
End each part with one thing to do, and say what it costs.
- Like this:  "Nothing is assumed, not even Node:  you need git, a GitHub login, and a terminal."
  And at the end of setup:  "That's it:  every command in Try it runs now."
- Not this:  "Get started in seconds!" over an install that takes minutes.
  Say "it takes minutes, not half a second" instead, and keep the reader's trust.

## 3. The shape of a page

A HOME PAGE (the product's front door):
1. **The line:**  what it is, in one sentence the reader could repeat.
2. **The proof:**  the thing working:  real code and output, a screenshot, a clip, captioned with where it came from.
3. **The doubt, answered:**  one or two sentences on the obvious suspicion.
4. **Try it:**  three to five things to do or see, each with its real result.
5. **Why you'd want it:**  what changes for the reader, each claim shown above or right here;  the unbuilt marked 🌱.
6. **What's in the box:**  the parts, in a picture or one table, each linking to its own page.
7. **Start here:**  the one next step, with its cost.
8. **🌱 Coming**, if anything is:  where it stands, no date.

A FEATURE PAGE is one feature, the same shape smaller:
1. **The line:**  what the feature does for the reader.
2. **The proof:**  it working, before and after when there is a before.
3. **How to use it:**  the steps, each with its result.
4. **The edges:**  what it won't do yet, in a plain sentence or two.
5. **Next:**  the page a reader would follow, and why.

A LAUNCH PAGE (something new shipped):
1. **The line:**  what's new, as what the reader can now do.
2. **The proof:**  it working.
3. **Try it now:**  the command, the link, the version to update to.
4. **What changed:**  each change the reader meets, one line each;  the full list folded or linked.
5. **What isn't in it:**  what people will ask about and won't find, said before they ask.
- It never narrates the work ("our team spent months ..."):  the reader came for what they get.

How long:
- A home page as long as its proof needs, and no longer:  the reader who stops after the first screen should know
  what it is, believe it's real and know the next step.
- Reference (every part, every setting) goes on the pages that own it, or folds (`<details>`);
  the page must read whole with every fold closed.

## 4. Seen through:  the symptoms and the cures

| Symptom | What it looks like | The cure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A tagline that begs | "English you can run.  Really." | Drop the begging word;  the proof under it does the convincing. |
| A promise with no proof | "Three commands cover everything" over a list of three names | Show one of them doing what the heading says, or soften the heading to what's shown. |
| The vision as the product | an "aspiration" section with nothing marked | Mark what isn't built 🌱, and point at the part they just saw working. |
| Adjectives for numbers | fast, powerful, seamless, effortless | The measured number, or cut the sentence. |
| The category, not the thing | "a platform", "a solution", "an ecosystem" | Say what it is, in the words the reader would use. |
| Selling the setup | "in seconds", "zero config", "just run" | Say what it takes, honestly:  the time, the tools, the login. |
| Narrating the page | "On this page you'll learn ..." | Delete it:  the headings do that job. |
| Narrating the makers | "We're excited to announce", "our journey" | What the reader can now do, and nothing about the mood of the team. |
| Insider words | internal project names, phase numbers, version code names | Say what it MEANS for the reader, or cut it. |
| The same pages twice | a table of pages, then a bullet list of them, then See also of them | One home per fact (section 5). |
| Notes for the builders | "Until X lands" folds, history of the layout | Move them to the builders' pages;  a product page is the product as it is. |

## 5. One home per fact

A product page goes stale fastest when the same list lives in three places.
- **One map of the parts:**  a picture or ONE table, the parts' own pages linked from it.
  No second list of the same parts below it, no "also beside it" line.
- **The home page owns** the one line of what it is, the proof, and the first step;
  every other page says a clause and links there.
- **A feature is explained on ONE page,** the one named for it;  others say a sentence and link.
- **See also is curated:**  at most five links, each with the reason you'd follow it.
- **A fact has one form:**  a number, a command, a price or a name is spelled the same everywhere,
  taken from the product or its output, not from another page.
- Two sections making the same pitch:  delete the weaker.

## 6. Rules that aren't taste

- **Never claim what doesn't run.**  What's coming is marked 🌱, says where it stands, and has no date.
- **Show only real output, real code and real screenshots:**  run it, or copy it from a file or a test snapshot.
  A mock-up is labelled as one.
- **Every picture has a caption** saying what it shows, and where it came from when that matters.
- **No testimonials, logos or numbers you can't source.**  A quote names who said it;  a count says what it counts.
- **"People", not "users",** in what people read.
- **Keep every fact** when rewriting:  every command, number, link, caveat and picture survives
  (reworded, moved to the page that owns it), or is cut ON PURPOSE for a reason you name:
  it repeated a fact stated elsewhere, it described how things used to be, it was for the builders, or it was wrong.
- **No file path in running prose:**  name the thing and link it;  a path in a code span or a table is fine.
- **Mechanics:**  the format the site uses.
  In Markdown, a fold is `<details>`, then `<summary>`, then a BLANK line, then the body.

## 7. How to work

1. Read the old page, and try the product the way the page says to:  run every command, open every link.
2. Plan the page in six words:  line, proof, doubt, try, why, next.
3. Check every claim against the product:  run it, read the file, count it.
   A claim you can't check is cut, or marked 🌱.
4. Write it.
5. Re-read it cold, top to bottom, as the reader in section 1, braced for selling.
   Mark every sentence that asks to be believed instead of showing something;  rewrite or cut each one.
   Then name the three weakest headings, the ones whose body doesn't keep their promise, and fix them.
6. Run the project's page checker, if it has one, and fix every finding.
7. Report:  the page, its lines before and after, the facts you cut on purpose and why, what you couldn't verify, and
   one honest sentence on where the page still sells instead of shows.

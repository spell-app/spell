
<div id="spell-app-root"><test-project-fizzbuzz-explainer><ui-container text=""><ui-stack><ui-header level="1">FizzBuzz</ui-header><ui-section label="The results"><ui-statistics size="mini" widths="4"><ui-statistic color="blue" label="fizz" value="0"></ui-statistic><ui-statistic color="green" label="buzz" value="0"></ui-statistic><ui-statistic color="purple" label="fizzbuzz" value="0"></ui-statistic><ui-statistic label="plain numbers" value="0"></ui-statistic></ui-statistics></ui-section><ui-section label="How it works"><ui-markdown>Count from 1 to 100, and say a word in place of some of the numbers:

- a number 3 goes into exactly:  **fizz**
- a number 5 goes into exactly:  **buzz**
- a number both go into, which is any number 15 goes into:  **fizzbuzz**
- every other number:  the number itself

The order of the checks matters:  15 is checked first, because 15 is a fizz and a buzz too.

This example's counting doesn't print anything.
It sends an event for each number, `fizz`, `buzz`, `fizzbuzz` or `plain number`, carrying the number,
and whoever is listening decides what to do with it:
the console prints a line, and this page adds a label.</ui-markdown></ui-section><ui-section label="Where it comes from"><ui-markdown>FizzBuzz began as a children's counting game in Britain, played to practise division:
players count in turn, and anyone who says a number instead of "fizz" or "buzz" is out.

It became famous among programmers in early 2007, as a test of whether someone can write code at all.
Imran Ghory suggested it as a quick screen for job interviews,
and Jeff Atwood's post "Why Can't Programmers.. Program?" spread the idea widely.
Since then it has been written in nearly every programming language.</ui-markdown></ui-section></ui-stack></ui-container></test-project-fizzbuzz-explainer></div>
